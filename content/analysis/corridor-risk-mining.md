Discussion of mining risk tends to concentrate on two things: the security of
the site itself, and the stability of the fiscal terms governing it. Both
matter. Neither, in our observation, is where most operators actually lose
money.

Across the jurisdictions we cover, the recurring pattern is that the asset
performs broadly as expected while the corridor connecting it to market does
not. Road conditions, informal levies, community blockades, border friction and
intermittent rail availability accumulate into a cost line that is rarely
reported as a single figure and is therefore rarely managed as one.

## Why it is underweighted

Part of the explanation is organisational. Site security and government
relations typically sit with different functions from logistics, and corridor
disruption is often absorbed within freight budgets rather than escalated as
risk.

Part of it is that corridor risk is undramatic. A blockade that closes a road
for two days does not generate the same attention as an incident at a mine
site, even where the cumulative effect of several such closures across a
quarter is larger.

The result is that a category of risk that is relatively predictable, and in
many cases mitigable, receives less structured attention than categories that
are neither.

## What can reasonably be anticipated

Corridor disruption is more forecastable than site-level security risk, which
is one reason we think the imbalance is worth correcting.

Community grievance processes generally have a visible history. Seasonal road
degradation follows a calendar. Fuel supply constraints and border staffing
changes are frequently signalled in advance. Where a corridor has been
disrupted before, the conditions that produced the disruption are usually still
present.

This does not make corridor risk trivial to manage. It does make it amenable to
planning in a way that is often not attempted.

## A note on fiscal terms

On the question that most often accompanies this one — whether fiscal terms are
likely to be reopened — we would offer a measured view.

Across the jurisdictions we currently assess, renegotiation of fiscal terms
appears considerably more likely than expropriation, and the two are sometimes
discussed as though they sat on the same spectrum. They do not. Renegotiation
is a commercial process with precedent, timelines and room for engagement.
Treating it as a precursor to expropriation tends to produce worse outcomes
than treating it as what it usually is.

We would revise that view on evidence of coordinated action across multiple
jurisdictions, or on changes to the constitutional treatment of resource
ownership. Neither is currently observable in the markets covered here.

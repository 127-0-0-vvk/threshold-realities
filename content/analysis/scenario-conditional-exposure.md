There is a durable commercial appetite for confident political prediction. We
understand its appeal, and we would gently suggest that it is usually an
appetite for reassurance rather than for information.

The difficulty is well documented and not particular to any one firm. Political
outcomes are produced by a large number of interacting factors, many of which
are unobservable in advance, and the record of expert prediction in this domain
is modest. We do not think this reflects a lack of rigour among those
attempting it. We think it reflects the nature of the problem.

## A more tractable question

The question clients actually need answered is rarely who will prevail. It is
what changes for them under each outcome, and how much warning they are likely
to have.

That question is considerably more tractable, for a straightforward reason: the
number of commercially distinguishable outcomes is usually far smaller than the
number of political ones.

A dozen plausible coalition configurations may reduce to three positions on the
regulatory question a client actually cares about. Once the set is defined that
way, exposure can be modelled under each, contingencies can be prepared, and
the analytic burden shifts from prediction to observation — which of these is
occurring, and what would tell us early.

## How we frame published work

For that reason we try to structure forward-looking work around triggers rather
than dates. A date is a claim about timing that we are usually not in a
position to make. A trigger is an observable condition that a client can
monitor independently of us.

We attach confidence to our judgements, and we publish the basis on which
confidence is assigned. Where sourcing is thin or a mechanism admits more than
one reading, we would rather say so than present a uniform tone of certainty
across work of uneven strength.

## On keeping the record

We review forward-looking work after the fact and record how the triggers
resolved, including where they resolved against our assessment.

We would not claim this is unusual in principle. Most analytic organisations
believe they do it. In practice it is done less often than it is described,
partly because the incentives run the other way, and partly because it requires
having written the original judgement precisely enough to be scored.

That last point is, we think, the substantive one. The discipline of writing a
judgement that can be evaluated afterwards tends to improve the judgement
itself, independently of whether anyone later evaluates it.

## What this means in practice

For clients, the implication is modest but worth stating. Work framed this way
will sometimes read as less decisive than work that is not. An assessment that
sets out three outcomes with conditions attached is less satisfying than one
that names a winner.

We would argue it is more useful, on the grounds that it can be acted on
incrementally and revised as conditions develop, rather than requiring a single
commitment made at the point of least information.

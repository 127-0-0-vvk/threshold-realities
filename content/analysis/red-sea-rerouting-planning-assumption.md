Commercial routing decisions in the Red Sea corridor have moved through a
familiar sequence over the past eighteen months. An initial period of
disruption prompted temporary diversions. Those diversions were extended. They
have now been incorporated into published schedules, and increasingly into the
network designs that sit behind them.

This is a meaningful change, and it is worth stating carefully. It does not
indicate a settled view among carriers that the corridor will remain
constrained indefinitely. It indicates something narrower: that the cost of
planning around the corridor has fallen below the cost of planning for its
return.

## What has actually changed

Three developments are observable rather than inferred.

Schedules published for the coming quarters increasingly show Cape routings as
the base case rather than as an exception applied after the fact. Vessel
deployment has adjusted accordingly, with tonnage committed to longer rotations.
And service contracts concluded in recent months have, in several cases, been
priced against the longer routing rather than against a resumption.

Each of these is a commercial decision taken under uncertainty. None of them
constitutes a forecast about the security environment.

## Why the distinction matters

It is tempting to read carrier behaviour as a proxy for expert judgement about
regional stability. We would advise against it.

Carriers are optimising for schedule reliability and asset utilisation across a
multi-year planning horizon. A carrier may hold a relatively constructive view
of the security picture and still commit to the longer routing, simply because
the cost of repeated mid-quarter changes exceeds the benefit of a shorter
transit that may not prove durable.

The signal in this behaviour is therefore about planning tolerance, not about
risk. Reading it as the latter has led some shippers to adopt more pessimistic
assumptions than the underlying picture supports.

## Where the commercial exposure now sits

For firms moving goods on this corridor, the practical exposures have shifted.

Inventory positions calibrated to the shorter transit are the most common source
of avoidable cost we encounter. Where safety stock was set against a
Suez-routed lead time and has not been revisited, the gap tends to surface as
expedited freight rather than as a stockout, which makes it easy to miss in
reporting.

Contract renewal timing is the second. Agreements concluded during the initial
disruption are beginning to reach renewal, and the rate environment at renewal
will differ materially depending on whether the market has begun to price a
partial normalisation.

Insurance remains the most responsive indicator and continues to move ahead of
freight rates. We regard the spread between the two as more informative than
either on its own.

## What would change our view

We would revise this assessment on evidence of sustained transit volumes
returning to the corridor over a full quarter, rather than on announcements of
intent; on war-risk premiums narrowing durably rather than episodically; and on
carriers restoring Suez routings to published schedules rather than offering
them as optional upgrades.

Absent those, we would encourage clients to treat the current network design as
the planning case through at least the next contracting cycle, while avoiding
the stronger conclusion that the corridor is closed as a matter of structure.

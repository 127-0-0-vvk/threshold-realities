Export controls are most often analysed as a legal development: a list is
published, entities are designated, compliance functions respond. That framing
is accurate, and for compliance purposes it is the correct one. For commercial
planning it tends to arrive late.

The observation we would offer is narrower and, we think, more useful. In
several recent cycles, measurable changes in procurement behaviour preceded
formal consultation by a meaningful margin. Where firms were positioned to read
those changes, the resulting decisions were better informed than those taken
after publication.

## What the behaviour looks like

The pattern is not dramatic, which is part of why it is easy to overlook.

Lead times on specific components extend without a corresponding change in
headline demand. Distributors become less willing to quote forward beyond a
certain horizon. Spot pricing on a narrow set of parts separates from the
broader category. Order books at tier-one purchasers show forward commitments
that are difficult to explain on demand grounds alone.

Individually, each has innocent explanations. Read together, and particularly
where they cluster around a component set that policy discussion has already
touched on, they merit attention.

## The limits of the signal

We would not present this as a predictive method, and we would caution against
treating it as one.

Procurement teams forward-buy for many reasons, and several of those reasons
have nothing to do with policy. Capacity constraints, currency positioning,
supplier consolidation and straightforward inventory cycling all produce
similar traces. A firm reading these signals in isolation will generate false
positives, some of them expensive.

What the signal supports is a change in posture rather than a change in plan:
bringing forward a dependency review, opening a conversation with an
alternative supplier, or simply establishing what the substitution options
would be before they are needed. These are low-regret actions. Committing
capital on the strength of the signal alone is not.

## Where the second-order exposure sits

Attention naturally concentrates on the firms directly named by a control
regime. In our experience the more consequential exposure is usually one or two
tiers below.

Tier-one purchasers typically have both the balance sheet to pre-buy and the
policy intelligence to know when to. Their suppliers frequently have neither.
The result is that a control regime absorbed comfortably at the top of a supply
chain can transmit with considerable force further down it, arriving at firms
that had no visibility of the policy discussion at all.

For companies whose exposure is indirect, the practical implication is that
monitoring the named entities is insufficient. The relevant question is which
of your suppliers depends on a component they cannot substitute, and whether
they have the balance sheet to absorb a disruption if one arrives.

## A note on mapping

Firms that identify this exposure early tend to share one habit: they map
dependency at the component level rather than at the supplier level.

A supplier map answers who you buy from. A component map answers what you
cannot readily replace. Only the second is useful when a control lands, and
building it is considerably less work than most firms assume before they
attempt it.

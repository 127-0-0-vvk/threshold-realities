A prohibition is, in commercial terms, a manageable thing. It can be modelled,
priced, and planned around. Counsel can advise on it with confidence, and a
board can take a decision knowing what it is deciding.

Ambiguity is harder. Where a licensing position is unsettled, or where the
interpretation of an existing position varies between agencies or over time,
firms are asked to commit capital against a rule they cannot yet state. Most
decline, and they decline quietly.

## Why the cost is difficult to observe

Standard risk reporting captures transactions that were blocked, delayed or
unwound. It does not capture transactions that were never brought forward,
because nothing was ever recorded.

In several markets we cover, we assess that the second category is
substantially the larger of the two. Investment committees decline to schedule
the discussion; business development teams direct attention to jurisdictions
where the position is clearer; a project remains at concept stage past the
point where it would have proceeded under a clear rule, restrictive or
otherwise.

None of this generates a data point. It is nonetheless a real allocation of
capital away from a market, and it is generally not reversed quickly once the
position clarifies.

## What tends to signal a change

Licensing positions rarely shift through clean announcements. In our
observation the earlier indicators are procedural rather than declaratory.

Changes in processing times for existing applications often precede changes in
policy. So does the appearance of new conditions attached to renewals, and
variation in how comparable applications are treated across a short period.
Public statements, where they come, frequently confirm a change already visible
in administration.

We would not overstate the reliability of these indicators. They are noisy, and
administrative variation has causes unrelated to policy. But they are
observable, which is more than can be said for most of what is written about
sanctions direction.

## The practical control

For firms with exposure to markets in this condition, the control that does
most work is unglamorous: counterparty and ownership mapping, built early and
maintained continuously.

It is worth adding one observation. Where ownership structures are opaque,
firms frequently treat that opacity as an obstacle to diligence — something to
be worked around in order to reach a conclusion. We would suggest treating it
as a finding in its own right. A counterparty whose ownership cannot be
established is a different proposition from one whose ownership is established
and acceptable, and the distinction is worth recording as such.
